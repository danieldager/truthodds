01What this is

The score is a sum over evidence documents. Each document contributes one number, the log ratio of how often a document like it turns up on a true claim versus a false one. The only design choice here is how finely we sort the documents into buckets before counting. Three versions of that choice are on this page. Three buckets, seven buckets, twenty eight buckets. Same claims, same documents, same estimator, same folds. Nothing else moves.

3 bucketssupport, refute, silent
7 bucketsthe reader's own flag, ungrouped
28 bucketsthe flag crossed with the source tier
3,274 claims1,502 true and 1,772 false
31,888 documentsfive folds, every number out of fold

The population is fc gold with two groups set aside. Media provenance claims are out, 336 of them, because there the fact checker graded whether a photo or video shows what it is presented as showing and our reader only ever sees text. The misleading group is out too, 425 claims where the verdict is about the post's framing or its missing context rather than about the claim we actually score. The 118 unprovable claims stay in. They count as false when we measure and they stay out of every fit, which is the standing convention.

Setting the misleading group aside is what moves the seven bucket number from 0.833 to 0.862. That is the same sensitivity cut already reported in the sprint, so this page and that row are the same measurement.

02The curves

Detection framing throughout. A false claim is the thing being detected, so recall runs over gold false claims and the false alarm rate runs over gold true ones. A claim scores low when the evidence goes against it, so we flag at or below a threshold.

Finer buckets win, and the win is small in rank terms and larger where we actually operate. Going from three buckets to seven is worth 1.0 points of AUC and 8.2 points of recall at the 2% budget. Going on to twenty eight adds 0.7 more points of AUC and only 0.7 of recall. Both intervals clear zero against the three bucket baseline, and they are paired, so the comparison is on the same resampled claims every time.

03The weights

Every bucket carries one weight. It is the log likelihood ratio for a single document in that bucket, smoothed by adding one imaginary document to every bucket in each class so an empty bucket cannot send a weight to infinity. No bucket borrows from any other. That is the point of the intervals. A wide bar means the bucket has thin evidence of its own, not that we shrank it toward something safer.

The bars are 95% bootstrap intervals, 2,000 replicates resampled inside each gold class, with the weights refit inside every replicate. A hollow dot marks a bucket with fewer than 30 documents on one side. Read those as placeholders rather than as findings.

Three things the collapse to three buckets hides. Flag 2, points against, is charged -0.70 on its own but pays the full refute weight of -1.82 once collapsed, so a weak counter signal is priced like a flat contradiction. Flag X, on claim context with no direction, comes out +0.30, mildly positive, and the collapse buries it in the silent channel at -0.14. And support splits hard by tier once you look, +3.67 for a rated outlet stating the claim against +1.96 for a primary source doing the same.

05The weights it learned instead

Same three panels as before. The dot and its interval are the urn fit. The tick is where the gold fit put the same bucket, so the distance between them is what changed when the labels went away.

Two things to read off it. The urn weights are compressed toward zero almost everywhere, which is expected because the false pile is one narrow slice of false claims and the true pile still has some false in it. Compression alone would not hurt a ranking. What does hurt is that the compression is uneven. Support falls from +2.19 to +1.06 while refute only falls from -1.82 to -1.18, so the balance between the two channels moves, and that does change the ranking.

The source tier panel is where it breaks. A reliable outlet stating the claim is worth +3.67 on gold and only +1.30 on the urns. An irrelevant document from a reliable outlet is -0.08 on gold and -0.75 on the urns. Those are not small disagreements and they are not noise, the intervals do not overlap. Source tier is picking up what each corpus retrieves rather than whether the claim is true, so it is exactly the feature that should not be expected to travel.

06Scoring the urns instead

Now the eval flips. The weights come from fc gold and the thing being scored is our own data, 1,719 community noted false claims against 1,999 timeline claims. There is one problem to deal with first. The timeline is not a clean true set, some share of it is false, so counting every timeline claim we flag as a false alarm undercounts how well the score is doing.

We correct it rather than ignore it. If a false claim sitting in the timeline scores like a false claim in the community notes urn, which is the same assumption the de-mixing already makes, then at any threshold the observed false alarm rate is the true one diluted by the contamination

FPR observed = (1 - eps) x FPR true + eps x recall

Rearranged, that corrects the whole curve point by point, and integrating the corrected curve gives AUC true = (AUC observed - eps/2) / (1 - eps). Both numbers are below. At an assumed false share of 10% the correction is worth about 2.9 points of AUC.

The reverse direction transfers cleanly. Fitted on gold and scored on the urns, seven buckets get 0.793 against 0.795 for the urns fitted on themselves. That is the same number. So gold labels teach the reader flags nothing corpus specific, while the reverse direction lost 1.5 points. The gold fit is the more general of the two, not just the better one on its own turf.

Twenty eight buckets are the exception again and in the direction the earlier sections predicted. On the urns, the urn fit beats the gold fit 0.806 against 0.782, which is what you expect if the tier cells encode each corpus's own retrieval mix. Whichever corpus you fit on, the tier split works at home and travels badly.

Pooling does not help. On gold it costs seven buckets 1.0 points against fitting on gold alone, 0.852 against 0.862. On the urns it matches the urn fit rather than beating it, 0.795 against 0.795. It is never the best cell in either column. What it does buy is insurance at twenty eight buckets, where pooling rescues the gold score from 0.823 to 0.850. So mixing the corpora is a hedge against a bad fit, not a way to a better one.

One sensitivity we ran and are not headlining. The band audit read the bottom 400 timeline claims by score and called 192 of them false. Dropping the 191 of those that match a scored claim by its text and re-scoring lifts the gold fit at seven buckets to 0.811. We do not report that as the number, because the audit only ever looked below a score cut, so removing claims exactly where the score already said false raises the result by construction. Treat it as an upper bound on what a fully audited timeline would give. The removed count is 191 rather than 192 because that join keys claims on their text and three of them collide on the key, which is also why the cross ladder's own bookkeeping records the audit as 397 claims and 190 falses. Those are join counts. The audit read 400 and called 192 false, and that is the pair to quote.

08The score against blind labels, on an unconditional draw

Every number above scores the ladder against labels that already existed. This section is the first time the score has been checked against labels collected on a draw that did not look at the score. 400 claims were sampled uniformly at random from the 1,999 scored timeline claims, seeded, before any score was consulted, and audited blind. The auditor saw the claim and the post it came from and nothing else. No score, no flag, no band, no retrieved document, no community note. It did its own web research and returned true, false or unverifiable.

That independence is the whole point. The band audit in section 07 read the bottom 400 claims by score, so a false rate measured on it is a property of the cut and cannot be read as a property of the timeline. This draw can.

On the first pass the false share is 0.086, 30 false against 319 true on the 349 claims that pass could decide. The exact 95% interval is [0.059, 0.120] and the post clustered one, which lets several claims from the same post move together instead of counting them as independent draws, is [0.053, 0.123]. Always quote the post clustered interval rather than the exact one. It is the wider of the two and it is the one that matches how the sample was actually built, 349 claims off 247 posts, and the design effect is 1.39 so ignoring the clustering would understate the width by about a fifth.

That first pass number is a floor, not the estimate. A second harder pass went back over the 51 claims the first pass could not settle and resolved 23 of them, and those came back 8 false against 15 true, a false share of 0.35 against 0.086 in the easy claims. Unsettleable claims are enriched in false ones, which is exactly the direction that makes dropping them from the denominator biased low. Folding every read in gives 0.102 on 372 decided claims, post clustered 95% [0.067, 0.141]. That is the number to carry. Every urn side figure on this page is corrected at an assumed 10%, which sits almost exactly on it, so the assumption this page has been running on for weeks turns out to be right and nothing above needs restating.

The unverifiable claims are the binding uncertainty, so they got their own pass. 51 of the 400 claims came back unverifiable on the first read, 12.8% of the draw. If every one of them were secretly true the false share would be 0.075 and if every one were secretly false it would be 0.203, a span of 12.8 points, which is far wider than the sampling interval. Another few hundred claims would not touch that, so the reads went here instead. All 51 were re-read by a fresh auditor working to a harder brief, told to escalate through archived copies, official registries, company and agency newsrooms, trade press and non English sources, and to distinguish no source found from no source can exist. That pass settled 23 of 51 and left 28 standing. The residual ambiguity band is [0.095, 0.165], a span of 7.0 points against 12.8 before, so the pass cut the binding uncertainty roughly in half.

Of the 28 that stayed unverifiable, 22 are hard but resolvable and 6 are structurally unresolvable. Hard but resolvable means a record that would settle the claim exists somewhere the auditor could not reach, behind a paywall, on a deleted or unarchived page, in a subscription database, or in a language or jurisdiction it could not search. Somebody with that access could settle it. Structurally unresolvable means no record that would settle it exists or could exist, because nobody wrote it down, or the referent is too vague to check, or it is about the future, or it is not a factual proposition at all. So only 21% of the residue is out of reach in principle and the other 79% is out of reach in practice, which says the ceiling here is access and effort rather than the nature of the claims. The shapes that dominate the residue are volatile ephemeral at 4, private or internal at 4, sources conflict at 4 and ordinary checkable at 3, with the rest spread thin over 7 other kinds.

The false calls were read twice. The estimator is more sensitive to a wrong false than to a wrong true, because a false sits in the numerator and a true only in the denominator, so every claim called false went to a second independent auditor who was not told what the first one said. 23 of 30 came back false again. On its own that means little, because a second reader who is simply more sceptical would also confirm them, so 69 claims called true were sent to a second auditor the same way without being marked as a control. 2 of those came back false. The two rates differ with a Fisher exact p of 2.6e-14 at an odds ratio of 110, so the second reader is agreeing with the first about which claims are false rather than being uniformly harsher. Without that control this arm would say nothing. The 7 falses the second reader did not confirm are real disagreement though, and they put a band on the numerator. Under the strictest rule, both readers must say false, the share is 0.066. Under the loosest, either reader saying false is enough, it is 0.112. Both sit inside the interval on the number the box above says to carry, so the reading rule is not what decides this.

What each column is. The s7 band is a range of the score itself, which is the sum of the log odds weights over one claim's retrieved documents, so a claim sits low when the evidence leans against it and high when it leans for it. Audited is how many of the 400 sampled claims landed in that band. True, false and unverifiable are what the blind auditor came back with. False share is false over true plus false, so the unverifiable claims leave the denominator, because we do not know which way they would have fallen. That number is eps, the same eps this page uses everywhere else for the share of the timeline that is really false. Beside it is a Clopper Pearson interval, which is a 95% confidence interval for a proportion that does not go through the normal approximation. The normal approximation needs a count big enough to look like a bell curve and returns nothing usable at two false claims out of five, where Clopper Pearson works straight off the binomial and still answers. It answers conservatively, so the interval is a little wider than it strictly needs to be. Claims in urn is how many of the 1,999 scored timeline claims sit in that band, share of urn is the same count as a percentage, and implied false takes the band's false share and applies it to every claim in the band, which makes it an extrapolation rather than a count.

What the first 40 claims did and did not show, because it matters for reading this table. The shakedown pass audited 40 claims and every one of the five bands came back holding exactly one false claim. That ladder was entirely the denominators moving, 2, 3, 3, 5 and 18 decided claims from the bottom band up, and the numerator carried no information at all. We checked it for a bug in the join between the audit labels and the bands and it came back clean, the draw reproduces element for element from its seed and the label join is one to one. Read against the 31 decided claims rather than all 40, the pattern is about 7.5 times more likely under a monotone model than under a flat one, which is weak evidence for the signal and a pattern we went looking for after seeing it. The 400 claim draw below replaces that read entirely. The numerator now moves across the bands and the table stands on its own.

What is real, and does not lean on the audit at all, is the right hand side of both tables. The band populations are ordered the way a working score orders them, 822 of the 1,999 claims in the top band down to 43 in the bottom one. That is the score doing its job on a population that is mostly true, and it is measured on all 1,999 claims rather than on 400.

Does the ladder survive at real n. Yes, and this is the question the whole exercise existed to answer. Under the two urn weights the false share falls across all five bands with no reversal, from 0.400 in the bottom band to 0.025 in the top one, and a trend test across the five ordered bands returns z equal to -4.45 with p equal to 0.0000. Under the gold weights the same test returns z equal to -5.38 and p equal to 0.0000. Collapsed to the single strongest two way split the two urn cut gives 0.135 on 192 decided claims below against 0.025 on 157 above, Fisher exact one sided p equal to 0.0001. That is the strongest of the four possible splits and it is quoted as such. The cleanest statement does not need bands at all. Ranking the 349 decided claims by score, a randomly chosen true claim scores above a randomly chosen false one 77.0% of the time under the gold weights and 73.7% under the two urn weights, and a permutation test that shuffles labels between whole posts rather than between claims returns p equal to 0.0000 and 0.0001. At 40 claims the strongest split reached p equal to 0.09 and we could say only that the direction was consistent. At 400 it is significant.

What that does and does not establish. It establishes that the score orders claims the same way a blind human style read does, on a draw that never saw the score. It does not say the score is calibrated, and the two tail bands still decide on a handful of claims each, so the per band rates in the extremes carry intervals wide enough to overlap their neighbours. The ordering is measured. The per band level is not.

What this does to the numbers above. Nothing, and that is worth stating rather than leaving implied. Every urn side figure on this page is corrected at an assumed false share of 10% and the best measured estimate is 10.2%. To put a bound on it anyway, re-running the whole cross ladder at the first pass share of 8.6% moves the headline cell, gold weights scored on the urns at seven buckets, from 0.7926 to 0.7859 and recall at a 2% false alarm budget from 24.2% to 21.0%. Swept from one end of the post clustered interval to the other the same cell runs 0.7760 to 0.7977, a 2.2 point span, against a bootstrap interval on that cell of [0.7748, 0.8086] which is already 3.4 points wide. The assumed share was never the binding uncertainty. The reason to measure it was that it had never been measured.

The first pass recorded its own coarser reason for each unverifiable claim, before the deeper pass reclassified them, and on that reading they were no public record 24, sources conflict 7, vague underspecified 5, media dependent 4, opinion not factual 4, record inaccessible 4, context dependent 2 and future or prediction 1. The two lists disagree mostly because no public record is what a normal search budget concludes and the deeper pass then found the record about half the time. That is the finding worth extending. The ceiling on this bucket is search effort, not the claims themselves.

09The claims that get nothing worth weighing

This section changes subject. Everything above is about how to weigh the documents a claim gets back. This is about the claims where there is nothing to weigh. A claim is in this block when every retrieved document read irrelevant, all ten of them, no support, no refutation, nothing on the claim at all. That is 494 of 1,772 fc gold false claims and 335 of 1,969 community noted ones.

It is not a coverage failure, which was the first thing to rule out. The documents come back at the normal rate, a median of 10 per claim in this block against 10 for the claims that worked, so nothing is being thrown away between the search and the read. The evidence is findable too. Take the gold claims here whose reviewer's own domain never appeared in any result at all, and 248 of 313 of them cite at least one source on a well indexed domain, meaning NewsGuard rated or government or academic or a primary source. The reviewer's evidence was sitting where a search engine can see it. We asked for something else.

So the next question is what the queries were doing wrong. Six hundred of them were labelled, 200 from each miss arm and 100 from a control arm of claims in the same corpus whose retrieval worked.

The control columns are the point. Most of these modes are just as common when retrieval works. over_generic runs 28.5% against 32.0% on the notes and lower than its own control on gold, and wrong_date is below its control in both corpora, so neither one is what separates a miss from a hit. The mode that rises in both is nonexistent_event, 41.0% against 28.0% on the notes and 48.0% against 37.0% on gold. The claim describes an event that never happened, so a query asking for it has nothing to match and the engine answers with whatever shares its words.

The tidy explanations are not the story. Wrong language, mangled names and unresolved deixis together are 2.5% of the notes arm and 5.0% of the gold arm. And in 14.5% of the gold misses the labeller found nothing wrong with the query at all, which is no better than its control. A rewriting pass has less to work with here than it looks.

This is the block that caps recall. We never flag on silence, so a claim whose ten documents are all off claim cannot be flagged no matter what the weights are. Every ladder on this page moves recall inside the claims that retrieve something. This block sits outside all of them, it is 28% of the gold false side and 17% of the community noted one, and it is the largest lever on recall we have identified.

The query labels are one LLM pass over the claim, its query and the ten domains that came back, so the split between over_generic and wrong_date is approximate. The four arms are sized by design rather than by prevalence, so read down each column and not across the bottom row.

10The evidence we know exists, and cannot reach

Section 09 measured the block. This section attacks it, six ways, and then works the problem backwards. The block is every false claim where all ten retrieved documents read irrelevant, 494 of 1,772 fc gold falses and 335 of 1,969 community noted ones, so roughly a quarter of what the tool exists to catch.

The reader is not the constraint, which had to be ruled out first. What follows, change one. Retrieve the record, not the claim

Add a step between the claim and the search that names what the claim implies rather than what it states. Which slot, which record, which canonical event name. Then search for that. For a quote attribution claim it is the transcript or the original remarks. For a statistic it is the dataset or the report the number was pulled from. This also needs a changed reading step, because our reader asks whether a document supports or refutes the claim, and a transcript showing what was actually said never mentions the claim at all. The right question for a retrieved record is whether it establishes what was the case, and whether that matches what was asserted.

What follows, change two. Stop excluding the origin source, selectively

22% of the reviewer's cited sources are the article the claim was lifted from. We exclude those by standing rule, and the rule is right when the question is whether something is true, because an outlet cannot corroborate itself. It is wrong when the question is what the source actually said, which is exactly the case when a claim is a misreading of a real report. Those are two different uses of the same document and we currently block both.

What this does not establish. The oracle is only as good as the reviewer's link set, and 77% of that link set turned out not to be evidence on its own. 96 of 219 targets could not be dated, mostly dead links. The query ladder ran on 55 claims, which is enough to rule out a large effect and not enough to rule out a small one. Neither change has been tested, and the measurement that matters for either is not whether it finds more documents but whether it finds more documents on false claims than on true ones. One earlier arm was killed for producing more refutations on true claims than on false ones. One further finding sits underneath all of this and neither change addresses it. With the date ceiling removed, 166 of 211 refuting documents postdate the claim, so part of this block is a latency problem rather than a reachability one, and a tool that must answer at post time cannot search its way out of that.
